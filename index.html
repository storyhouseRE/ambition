Her story is extraordinary. Drug-dealer parents. A sister lost. Melanoma. $700K in liens. A 10,000 sq ft house GC'd with a newborn. That is exactly why the trap is dangerous for HER specifically.

Their life → Your story → The idea → Back to their life
  1. OPEN in THEIR world. (Not your story. Theirs.)
  2. Take them into YOUR story.
  3. Extract THE IDEA.
  4. Land it back in THEIR world.
  5. CLOSE in THEIR life. Not Sasha's.

The story is PROOF of the idea. It is not the point of the talk.

📏 The rules

  1. ONE idea taken all the way down — not a framework overview.
  2. The story is PROOF of the idea — not the point.
  3. The audience must leave with something USABLE.
  4. Nearly fully scripted. Timing is everything.
  5. The title is everything — searchable, surprising, under 10 words.
  6. Slides sparingly — only when the visual does what words cannot.

🏆 "THE NEXT STEP" — the full script · ~2,200 words · 14–16 minutes
OPENING — Minutes 0–2
Start center stage. Slides begin immediately — childhood photos, candid and slightly chaotic.

I want to start by telling you something about where I came from.

[Photo: childhood home or neighborhood]

I grew up in a house where the entrepreneurial spirit was very much alive.

[Photo: parents, young Sasha]

My parents were self-employed. They worked from home. They had a loyal customer base, repeat business, a very efficient distribution model, and zero overhead.

They were drug dealers.

[Pause for laugh.]

I say that with love. And a little therapy. But here is the thing — and I mean this — they were my first entrepreneurs. Hustle, customer relationships, cash flow management. They had it. Just in a direction I chose not to follow.

[Photo: young Sasha — awkward, maybe school photo]

I was not the most popular kid. I was introverted. I liked computers and books and being alone and I was not exactly the girl anyone would have looked at and said — that one. She's going to build something.

And yet — here is something I did that changed the entire trajectory of my life.

I decided to become someone else.

Not forever. Not authentically. But I looked at the version of me that the world seemed to reward — outgoing, gregarious, top of the room — and I decided to perform that person until something happened.

I became the number one salesperson in every room I walked into.

I am also — I want you to know — still deeply, profoundly introverted. I would rather be at a computer. Alone. With my chickens.

[Pause.]

But I made a decision. About who I was going to be. About what I was going to allow to define me. About what I was going to let set me back.

And that decision — made by a girl with every statistical reason to fail — is what this talk is about.

THE IDEA — Minutes 2–4
Slides pause. Just you.

Here is what I want to challenge today.

We spend an enormous amount of energy waiting to feel ready. We wait for the right time. The right conditions. The right amount of experience, money, confidence, clarity.

We treat readiness like it's a destination — like someday, if we prepare enough and wait long enough, we will finally arrive at the version of ourselves that is qualified to begin.

I want to offer you something different.

You were born ready.

Not ready for everything. Not without fear. Not without uncertainty or self-doubt or the very reasonable feeling that you have no idea what you're doing.

Ready anyway.

Here's a question. Have you ever felt your heart pounding, throat tightening, stomach dropping, adrenaline flooding your whole body — and called it fear?

[Pause.]

What if I told you that is the exact same physiological experience as excitement?

Identical. Same heart rate. Same chemicals. Same body. The only difference is the story you tell yourself about what it means.

Some people feel that sensation before they walk on a stage and think: I'm going to die.

Other people feel the exact same thing and think: I am so alive right now.

You get to choose which story that is. You always have.

And that choice — what you decide to let define you, what you decide to call fear, what you decide you need in place before you're allowed to begin — that is your identity. And identities are not assigned. They are chosen.

Here is one more thing I want you to hear before I tell you what happened to me.

We talk a lot about discipline like it's something some people have and others don't. Like it's a personality trait you either got or you missed.

That is not what discipline is.

You are one hundred percent disciplined. Right now. Today.

You are disciplined to your current habits. Every single one of them. If you haven't worked out in six months — you are perfectly disciplined around not working out. If you smoke a pack a day — you are one of the most disciplined people I have ever met about smoking.

Discipline is not the problem. Direction is.

So. Who do you want to be? What story do you want that sensation in your chest to mean?

Decide. Because the rest of what I'm about to tell you only works if you do.

2015 — Minutes 4–5:30
Fast. List format. Almost breathless. Let the audience feel the weight of accumulation.

In 2015 I turned thirty-something and the world decided to test me.

In one calendar year —

I was diagnosed with melanoma.
I went through infertility treatment.
I had a miscarriage.
My sister died.
My husband was seriously injured in a work accident.
Someone close to me went through a divorce that exploded into our lives.

[Pause. Let it land.]

And I had the best professional year of my entire career.

[Beat.]

I know. I didn't understand it either at first.

But here is what I figured out somewhere in the middle of that year when I had nothing left — no plan, no roadmap, no emotional reserves, no capacity to think about what came next week let alone next quarter —

I couldn't control any of the things that mattered most to me.

I couldn't plan my way through grief. I couldn't roadmap my way through a cancer diagnosis. I couldn't wait for better conditions, because the conditions were not getting better.

All I could do — all any of us can do when everything is on fire — is find the very next available step.

And take it.

Not the plan. Not the five-year vision. Not the fully developed strategy with contingencies and backup contingencies.

Just the step.

So that is what I did. One step at a time, through the hardest year of my life. And it worked.

THE PRINCIPLE — Minutes 5:30–7

I want to name something clearly right now, because I know what you might be thinking.

You might be thinking this is a talk about resilience. About grit. About white-knuckling your way through hard things because that's what strong people do.

It is not.

I am not here to tell you to suffer more gracefully. I am not a grit coach. There is nothing admirable about pain for the sake of pain.

What I am telling you is something much more practical than that.

When the plan is gone and the conditions are impossible and you cannot see the finish line from where you're standing — there is always one thing available to you.

One phone call you haven't made.
One email you haven't sent.
One conversation you keep postponing.
One step that is sitting right in front of you, waiting.

Take that one.

That's it. That is the entire strategy.

And here is what I know after watching this work in my own life more times than I can count:

Motion creates momentum. And momentum — once you have it — is genuinely self-sustaining.

This is not motivation speaking. This is Newton's First Law. Objects in motion stay in motion. Not because the path gets easier. Not because the obstacles disappear. Because momentum, once built through daily committed forward motion, becomes the most reliable force in your life.

The step is always there. Even when nothing else is.

THE HOME BUILD — Minutes 7–11
Slide: house at 40% complete — raw, exposed framing, unfinished.

Five years after 2015, the world tested me again. And this time it brought reinforcements.

In 2018 my husband Kelley and I decided to build our dream home. We had found our lot, designed every detail, hired a builder with an impeccable reputation — someone I had worked alongside professionally for years and trusted completely.

And then Covid happened.

[Let that land.]

What we didn't know — what nobody told us — was that our builder was quietly falling apart. Twenty-three families were waiting on homes. Contractors were going unpaid. His pricing model had never been updated for what Covid had done to construction costs.

And somewhere in the middle of all of that, a few small floor plan changes we had requested cascaded through all three stories of the home. What we thought was a modest adjustment had quietly turned our build into a ten-thousand square foot custom home. Still priced on a pre-Covid contract.

When our builder filed for bankruptcy, he left behind twenty-three devastated families and our home — forty percent complete — with seven hundred thousand dollars in outstanding liens from contractors who had done the work and never been paid.

Every builder in the market was overbooked. Nobody wanted to touch a mid-construction project of that size and complexity.

So we decided to finish it ourselves.

[Pause.]

I want to be honest with you. This was not a heroic decision. This was two people with very few options deciding to keep moving.

We had no idea what we were doing.

We learned what a draw schedule was. We learned what a punch list was. We learned what a stop work order was — the hard way — when a subcontractor showed up on a Sunday, got reported to the county by a neighbor, and we lost a full month of progress.

We discovered the lot was sitting on two hundred tons of solid granite.

Which had to be dynamited.

[Pause for laugh.]

After the gorgeous, expensive, permanent metal roof had been installed — someone noticed that not a single bathroom in the entire house had ever been vented to the outside. Which meant the roof had to come partially off. And then go back on.

I started having nightmares about my newborn son falling through the open staircase hole that sat unfinished for weeks. I became superstitious. I refused to bring him to the property.

5 · It gets lonely — really lonely

Not lonely because you’re alone — because the further you go, the fewer people understand what you carry. And the local ones? You almost can’t tell them you’re struggling — they’re your clients, they see you as the one who has it together. So you carry it.

“It doesn’t mean you’re on the wrong path. The answer isn’t to shrink so you fit back in — it’s to find your people. Better rooms, not fewer ambitions.”
